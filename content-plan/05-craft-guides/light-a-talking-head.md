# How to light a talking head video at home

**Good talking head lighting needs one large soft source on your face and nothing fighting it. The easiest version is a window 45 degrees to one side as the key light, a white wall or a lamp with a similar color temperature on the opposite side as fill, every other light in the room off, and your phone's exposure and focus locked on your face. This costs nothing and looks better than a ring light aimed straight on.**

## Why it matters

Lighting decides skin tone, contrast, and whether the viewer's eye goes to your face. Flat overhead light makes eye sockets dark. Mixed sources make skin orange on one side and blue on the other. Both are visible within a second and neither is fixable later without damage.

## The method

1. **Find the biggest soft source.** A window in daytime, not in direct sun. If the sun is hitting the window, hang a white sheet over it.
2. **Put the source 45 degrees off your face.** Not straight on (flat), not behind you (silhouette). Slightly above eye level.
3. **Add fill on the other side.** A white wall, a foam board, or a lamp with a daylight bulb. Fill should be dimmer than the key, roughly half.
4. **Turn off everything else.** Overhead lights, warm lamps, the screen glow. Mixed color temperatures are the most common amateur tell.
5. **Add separation if you can.** A small lamp behind and to one side of you, pointed at your hair or shoulder, separates you from the background.
6. **Lock exposure and focus.** Tap and hold on your face in the phone camera until the lock appears. Otherwise the phone re-exposes every time you move.
7. **Check skin tone on the screen.** If you look orange, lower the warmth or move away from the warm lamp. If you look blue, the opposite.

## Rules of thumb

| Setting | Target |
| --- | --- |
| Key light angle | 30 to 45 degrees to one side |
| Key light height | slightly above eye level |
| Fill brightness | about half the key |
| Color temperature | one only, 5000 to 5600 K daytime, 3200 K if all lamps |
| Distance from key to face | 1 to 1.5 m for a window, 0.5 to 1 m for a lamp |
| Background light | separate small source, dimmer than the key |
| Phone exposure | locked on face, slightly under rather than over |

Night rule: if there is no daylight, use two lamps with the same bulbs, one as key at 45 degrees, one as fill at half distance further away, and turn off the ceiling light.

## Examples

Daytime desk setup: window camera-left at 45 degrees, white foam board camera-right, overhead off, phone locked on face. The setup used in most @sulansart studio videos where the window doubles as the studio light.

Evening setup: floor lamp with a daylight bulb camera-left at 1 m, bounce off a white wall camera-right, small desk lamp behind on the bookshelf for depth.

Ring light done right: ring light off-axis at 45 degrees, not straight on, dimmed to 40 percent, and the phone not mounted in its center. The center-mount is what causes the flat look and the ring reflection in the eyes.

## Common mistakes

- Window behind you. You become a silhouette.
- Ring light straight on. Flat face, ring in the eyes.
- Ceiling light plus a warm lamp plus a window. Three color temperatures on one face.
- Auto exposure. The image pulses every time you gesture.
- Overexposing to look "bright." Blown highlights on skin do not recover.

## FAQ

**Do I need to buy a light?**
Not for daytime. A window and a white surface cover it. For evening, one LED panel or a lamp with a daylight bulb is enough.

**Why does my face look orange?**
A warm lamp is mixing with daylight, or white balance is set wrong. Turn off the lamp or set white balance manually to match the dominant source.

**What about glasses glare?**
Raise the key light higher and move it further to the side. Tilt the glasses down slightly. Avoid straight-on sources completely.
